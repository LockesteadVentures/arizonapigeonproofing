A widely cited NREL finding says rain alone doesn't reliably clear dust and pollen buildup from solar panels — the light film just sits there through storms that would wash a windshield clean. In Arizona, where dust storms and heavy spring pollen are routine and rain is not, that film settles in and stays longer than it would almost anywhere else. Studies on arid-climate soiling have found meaningful production loss from heavy buildup, though the exact number for any given roof depends on the panel, the tilt angle, and how long the grime has been sitting there.

## Why rain doesn't fix this in the desert

Most people assume a good storm resets their panels the way it rinses a car. That's the intuition NREL's research pushes back on. Dust and pollen don't wash off the way a fresh coat of surface dirt does — they settle into a thin, sticky film, and a quick rain often just wets that film and redistributes it rather than carrying it away. You end up with streaks and a slightly muddier haze instead of a clean panel.

In wetter climates, this matters less because it rains often enough that even an imperfect rinse happens regularly. In Tucson, a heavy rain might come once every few weeks during monsoon season and then not again for months. Whatever dust settled in between doesn't get a second chance to wash off until the next storm, if it washes off at all.

## Why Arizona's dry climate makes it worse

Three things compound here that don't happen together in most parts of the country:

**Dust storms.** Haboobs kick up fine desert particulate that settles on every flat and tilted surface in the area, panels included. It happens fast, it's widespread, and it recoats a panel that was clean a day earlier.

**Heavy spring pollen.** Mesquite, palo verde, and olive pollen blanket outdoor surfaces for weeks each spring. It's sticky by nature, which is exactly why rain struggles to lift it back off the glass.

**Long dry stretches.** Between monsoon season and the occasional winter rain, there can be months with no meaningful precipitation at all. Whatever settles on the panels during that stretch just accumulates, layer on layer, until something actually washes it off.

That combination is specific to the desert Southwest. A panel in Seattle deals with pollen too, but frequent rain keeps knocking a share of it loose. A panel in Tucson can go most of a season without that reset.

## What the film actually does to output

Solar cells convert light into electricity, and a dust or pollen film sits between the sun and the cell, absorbing or scattering some of that light before it ever reaches the surface. Unlike a bird dropping, which blocks a concentrated patch and can create uneven cell-level stress, dust settles in a roughly even haze across the whole panel. Every cell loses a similar sliver of light, so the panel as a whole produces a bit less than it's rated for — without any single dramatic failure point. Studies on arid-climate installations have documented real, measurable losses from heavy accumulated soiling, but we're not going to hand you a specific percentage for your roof. That number depends on panel angle, local dust exposure, how long since the last real clean, and factors we can't estimate without seeing the array in person.

The practical sign is usually gradual: your production numbers trend down over weeks or months rather than dropping all at once, and there's no obvious single cause like a tripped breaker or a shading tree. ## Dust isn't the only thing that blocks light

If your panels have visible clumps or streaks rather than an even haze, you might be looking at something other than dust. Pigeons roosting above an array leave concentrated droppings that block light unevenly and can stress individual cells more than a uniform layer of grime. ## FAQ

**Does rain clean solar panels in Arizona?**
Not reliably. NREL's research found rain alone doesn't consistently clear dust and pollen film from panels, and in Arizona the dry stretches between storms give that film plenty of time to build up further before the next rain even has a chance.

**How much solar output do I actually lose from dust?**
It varies by panel angle, dust exposure, and how long since the last cleaning, so there's no single figure that applies to every roof. What's well established is that heavy soiling in arid climates causes real, measurable production loss — enough that it's worth checking your production trend rather than assuming rain has it handled.

**How often should panels be cleaned in Arizona because of dust and pollen?**
It depends on your specific location and roof exposure, but scheduling around spring pollen season and monsoon dust storms — rather than waiting until output visibly drops — tends to keep buildup from accumulating for months at a stretch.

**Is dust buildup the same problem as pigeon droppings?**
No. Dust and pollen settle as an even film across the whole panel, while droppings concentrate on specific cells and can cause uneven heating. Both block light and both matter, but they're different problems with different signs to look for.
