You need to clean your solar panels when you see a visible dust or pollen film, your monitoring app shows production down from the same period last year, it's been about a full season since the last wash, a monsoon storm just blew through, or you spot bird droppings and debris on the glass. Any one of these on its own is worth a look. Two or more together means don't wait.

Here's the thing most homeowners get wrong: panels can look fine from the ground and still be losing you real output. Dust doesn't always show up as an obvious gray haze — sometimes it's a fine, even film that just dulls the glass a shade, easy to miss unless you're looking for it. That's why this checklist leans on more than eyesight.

## The checklist

**1. A visible film or streaking you can see from the ground.** Stand in your yard, look up at the array in direct light (early morning or late afternoon works best — the low angle catches dust that midday sun washes out). If the panels look chalky, hazy, or streaked instead of glossy black, that's soiling. In Tucson, this builds fast because we're dealing with fine desert dust and, depending on the season, a heavy pollen load from mesquite and palo verde.

**2. Your monitoring app shows a real drop, not a seasonal one.** Every solar system loses some output in summer heat and gains some in cooler months — that's normal panel physics, not soiling. What you're looking for is a drop compared to the same month last year, or a decline that doesn't track with weather. If August 2026 production is meaningfully behind August 2025 on comparable sunny days, soiling is a likely culprit.

**3. It's been roughly a full season since the last cleaning.** Arizona's dust and pollen don't wait for a convenient time. If you can't remember the last time your panels were washed, or you're counting in seasons rather than months, that alone is reason enough to check.

**4. Right after a monsoon dust storm.** Monsoon season kicks up haboobs that coat everything outdoors, panels included. The rain that sometimes follows doesn't fix it — NREL research has found that rainfall alone doesn't reliably clear pollen and fine dust off solar glass. It can even streak dust into a thin film that clings harder than before. If you've been through a dust storm in the last few weeks, assume the panels need attention regardless of how they look from the ground.

**5. Visible bird droppings or debris.** Even a few droppings matter more than their size suggests. Bird waste blocks light unevenly, which can create hot spots on that section of the panel and drag down the whole string's output, not just the shaded cell. If you're seeing droppings, it's also worth asking whether birds are roosting on or under the array — that's a separate problem cleaning alone won't solve.

## Why looking isn't enough

A panel can appear clean and still be underperforming. Fine dust and pollen film build up in a way that's genuinely hard to see from ground level, especially on panels tilted at an angle where the sun doesn't hit them directly. The only reliable way to catch that kind of loss is checking your monitoring data against a real baseline — last year, or a similar sunny stretch a few months back. If you don't have monitoring, or you're not sure how to read it, a technician doing an on-site inspection can usually tell from panel temperature and output pattern whether soiling is the issue.

## FAQ

**How often should solar panels be cleaned in Arizona?**
Most Tucson-area systems benefit from cleaning roughly once a season, with extra attention after a monsoon dust storm. Your actual interval depends on your roof angle, nearby trees, and how much monsoon dust you get in a given year.

**Can I just check my panels visually to know if they need cleaning?**
Not reliably. Visual inspection catches obvious grime and streaking, but fine dust and pollen film can cut output noticeably before it's visible from the ground. Comparing your monitoring app's production to the same period last year is a better signal.

**Does rain clean solar panels enough on its own?**
No. NREL research has found that rain alone doesn't adequately clear pollen and fine dust from solar panels, and it can sometimes streak dust rather than remove it.

**Do bird droppings really affect solar panel output that much?**
Yes, more than their size suggests. Droppings block light unevenly, which can create hot spots and drag down output for the whole string of panels, not just the covered cell.
