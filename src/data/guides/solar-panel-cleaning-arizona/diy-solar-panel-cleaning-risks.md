A light rinse with a garden hose on a cool morning is low-risk and can help with dust between deeper cleanings. Past that, DIY solar panel cleaning risks start stacking up fast: falling off a roof that's steeper or hotter than it looks, scratching the anti-reflective coating with the wrong brush, cracking a panel with thermal shock, leaving hard-water streaks that cut output, and voiding your manufacturer's warranty by cleaning it a way the paperwork doesn't allow. None of that is meant to scare you off doing anything yourself. It's meant to draw an honest line between what's reasonable and what isn't.

## The roof itself is the biggest risk

Most panel-related injuries have nothing to do with the panels. They're falls. Tucson roofs run steep pitches, and panels themselves are slick when wet, mounted on racking that isn't built to be walked around on with a hose in one hand. Add a ladder set on uneven desert soil and a homeowner who's done this once a year, and the math isn't in your favor. A professional crew brings harness anchors, roof-rated footwear, and the muscle memory of doing this every week. If your roof access is easy and low, a quick rinse from ground level with an extension wand is a different risk profile entirely than climbing up there yourself.

## Scratching the glass you're trying to clean

Solar panels have a thin anti-reflective coating on the glass that helps them absorb more light. It's durable against weather, not against abrasion. Stiff-bristle brushes, kitchen scrub pads, or anything with grit will dull that coating over years of use, and once it's scratched, it doesn't heal. Hard water makes this worse in a sneaky way: mineral deposits that dry on the glass etch into the surface if they're scrubbed at instead of soaked and rinsed clean. A soft, non-abrasive squeegee or a plain hose spray avoids this. A car wash brush from the garage does not.

## Thermal shock: cold water, hot Arizona glass

This is the risk most DIY guides skip. Panel surface temperatures in direct Tucson sun regularly climb well above 140°F. Hit that glass with cold hose water and you create a rapid temperature swing across the panel's surface. Tempered glass is built to handle heat, not a sudden shock front between hot glass and cold water. Enough of that stress, especially repeated over seasons, and you get micro-cracking that shows up later as reduced output or a dead cell string, long after the cleaning day itself. The fix is simple: never clean panels in the middle of a Tucson afternoon. Early morning, before the glass has had hours to bake, is the only time of day this is safe to do at all.

## Why tap water leaves streaks, and deionized water doesn't

Tucson tap water carries dissolved minerals, mostly calcium and magnesium. When that water evaporates off a panel, the minerals don't evaporate with it. They stay behind as a thin film, which is exactly what shows up as streaking or a cloudy haze after a few cleanings. That film also blocks light, quietly cutting output in a way that's easy to miss until you compare bills. Deionized water has had those minerals stripped out before it ever touches the glass, so it evaporates clean with nothing left behind — see our [deionized water vs. pressure washing](/guides/solar-panel-cleaning-arizona/deionized-water-vs-pressure-washing/) comparison for how it actually stacks up. That's the whole reason professional cleaning services use it instead of a garden hose. It's also worth noting, per NREL's research, that rain alone doesn't reliably clear pollen and dust off panels the way people assume it does. So panels do need active cleaning; the question is just what kind of water you're using to do it.

## Warranty language most people never read

Panel manufacturers write cleaning instructions into their warranty documents, and they vary by brand: some prohibit pressure washers outright, some specify water hardness limits, some require ground-fault protection during any wet cleaning. Clean your panels a way that contradicts that document, and a manufacturer can point to it if you ever file a claim for an unrelated defect. Read your specific panel's warranty before you do anything more than a plain-water rinse.

## Where DIY is genuinely fine

To be fair to the DIY approach: a gentle hose rinse at dawn or dusk, no brush, no soap, just water knocking off loose surface dust, is low-risk and reasonable for light buildup between real cleanings. It won't touch bird droppings, hard-water spots already baked on, or anything requiring you to get on the roof. That's where it stops being a DIY job and starts being a professional one.

## FAQ

**Can I use a pressure washer on solar panels?**
Most manufacturers advise against it or restrict the pressure and nozzle type. High pressure can force water under panel seals or crack glass, and it often voids the warranty outright.

**Is it safe to clean solar panels myself if I have a single-story roof?**
It's lower-risk than a steep two-story roof, but the scratching, thermal shock, and streaking risks are the same regardless of roof height.

**How often do solar panels actually need cleaning in Tucson?**
It depends on dust, pollen season, and proximity to agriculture or unpaved roads, but most Tucson homes benefit from a professional deionized-water clean once or twice a year, with light hose rinses in between if convenient.
