Monsoon season doesn't clean your solar panels — it dirties them twice. First a haboob dumps a layer of fine grit on the glass, then the rain that follows turns that grit into a streaked film that clings harder than the dry dust did. If you assumed the rain would rinse things off, expect lower output through late summer instead, not higher.

## The dust arrives before the rain does

A haboob is a wall of dust kicked up by outflow winds from a collapsing thunderstorm, and it usually reaches your roof well ahead of any actual rainfall — sometimes by minutes, sometimes by an hour or more if the storm never drops rain on your neighborhood at all. That wind-driven dust isn't the fine haze that settles on panels during a calm week. It's coarser, heavier, and it gets pushed into every gap between panel and frame. On a Tucson roof, a single haboob can leave more visible grit than a month of normal dust accumulation.

That timing gap matters. A lot of Tucson homeowners see the dust cloud roll through, shrug, and figure the storm behind it will rinse the panels clean. Sometimes it does, for a day. Often it doesn't work that way at all.

## Why the rain doesn't finish the job

This is the part that catches people off guard: light-to-moderate monsoon rain falling on a dust-coated panel usually doesn't wash the dust away. It wets it, drags it downward in streaks, and lets it dry in place as a thin mud film baked on by the next afternoon's sun. NREL research on solar panel soiling backs this up directly — rain alone is not a reliable way to clear dust and pollen from panels, and a light rain on a dirty panel can leave the surface worse off than before it rained, not better. A panel that looked merely dusty in June can look genuinely streaked and blotchy by August, and that streaking blocks more sunlight than the original dry dust did.

The panels most exposed to wind-driven grit — a low-pitched roof facing the prevailing storm direction, or an array near open desert lots — tend to show this the worst. If your system's monitoring app shows a dip in daily output starting mid-monsoon that doesn't bounce back after a rain event, mud-streaking is the likely reason, not a wiring or inverter issue.

## Debris is the other monsoon risk

Wind is the other half of the monsoon equation. Gusts ahead of a storm cell can snap palm fronds, strip mesquite branches, and send roof gravel skittering across an array. None of that is catastrophic on its own, but debris sitting on a panel creates a shadowed hot spot, and grit trapped against the frame edge can work into mounting hardware over a full season. Worth a quick visual check after any big blow-through — from the ground with binoculars is fine, no need to get on the roof yourself.

## The timing that actually works

Given both halves of this problem, the practical fix is to schedule cleaning around monsoon season rather than during it:

- **Before monsoon season starts (May–June):** Clean panels so they go into the season already free of accumulated pollen and pre-monsoon dust. A clean starting surface sheds a haboob's grit more easily than one that already has a dust base for the wind to grip.
- **After monsoon season settles (September–October):** Clean again to remove the mud-streaking that built up over the wet months. This is usually the single most valuable cleaning of the year, since it's clearing months of baked-on residue rather than a light seasonal film.

Chasing every individual storm with a cleaning isn't practical or necessary — the pattern above catches the two points where dirt actually accumulates into an output problem.

## What actually gets the film off

Because the residue by late monsoon season is dried mud rather than loose dust, a hose rinse from the ground typically isn't enough — it just re-wets the film without lifting it. A deionized-water wash designed to leave panels spot-free without streaking handles the baked-on residue without leaving mineral spots of its own behind (an ordinary garden hose in Tucson's hard water can actually add new spotting even as it removes old grime).

## FAQ

**Does rain clean solar panels during monsoon season?**
Not reliably. Light-to-moderate rain on dust-coated panels tends to streak the dust into a mud film rather than rinsing it away. NREL's research on panel soiling confirms rain alone isn't an adequate cleaning method for dust and pollen buildup.

**How often should solar panels be cleaned during Arizona's monsoon?**
Most Tucson homeowners do best with two cleanings tied to the season: once before monsoon season starts, to begin with a clean surface, and once after it ends, to remove the mud-streaking that built up over the wet months.

**Can monsoon wind damage solar panels directly?**
Wind itself rarely damages panels, but wind-driven debris — branches, gravel, loose roofing material — can create shadowed hot spots or work into mounting hardware. A quick visual check after major storms is worth the five minutes.
