## Why the angle changes the physics

Solar panels don't shed debris the way a car windshield does when you're driving. They sit still, exposed to whatever the wind and rain bring, and what happens next comes down to two forces: gravity and water runoff.

On a steep tilt, gravity is doing real work on loose particles. Dust that lands on the surface has less to hold onto before it slides toward the bottom edge. Rain that does fall runs off faster and at more of an angle, carrying some of that loose debris with it, even if it's not a perfect rinse. It's not that steep panels stay clean on their own. It's that they lose a portion of what lands on them between cleanings, so the film that builds up is thinner and slower to form.

On a low-tilt or flat-mounted array, that self-clearing effect mostly disappears. Dust settles and just sits there. There's no gravity assist worth mentioning, and when rain does fall, it tends to pool briefly and evaporate rather than sheet off, which is exactly the setup that leaves streaks and a baked-in haze instead of a wash. This lines up with the broader point NREL has made about rain and solar glass: precipitation alone doesn't reliably clear pollen and fine dust, and on a flatter surface that unreliability gets worse, not better.

## Why this matters more in Tucson than most places

A lot of single-story homes around Tucson, Oro Valley, and similar neighborhoods have roof pitches on the low end, sometimes close to flat by solar standards, because that's simply how the house was built. Pair that with the region's dust storms and heavy spring pollen, and a low-tilt array here is accumulating buildup on two fronts at once: more dust in the air to begin with, and less angle to help shed it once it lands.

That doesn't mean a steeply pitched roof is exempt. Steep arrays in Tucson still soil up during monsoon season and pollen season, and they still need regular cleaning. The difference is one of degree and pace, not whether cleaning is needed at all. A steep array might go longer between visible film buildup than a flat one exposed to the same weather.

## What this means for judging your own schedule

Tilt angle is one input into how often your specific roof needs attention, not the whole answer. If your panels sit at a low angle, roughly 10 degrees or flatter, treat that as a signal to check them more often than you might otherwise assume, especially after a dust storm or during peak pollen weeks. If your array is mounted at a steeper pitch, you likely have a bit more room between cleanings, but "more room" doesn't mean "no schedule."

Rather than trying to convert tilt angle into a specific number of cleanings per year, the more reliable approach is watching the actual signs on your specific roof — visible film, a production dip in your monitoring app compared to the same period last year, or a recent dust storm. ## FAQ

**Do flat solar panels get dirtier faster than tilted ones?**
Yes. Flatter panels lack the gravity and runoff advantage that steeper panels get, so dust and pollen settle in and stay rather than partially clearing on their own between storms.

**What tilt angle is considered low for solar panels?**
There's no single industry cutoff, but arrays mounted at roughly 10 degrees or flatter behave more like a flat surface for dust purposes than a sloped one, and tend to need closer attention.

**Does a steep tilt mean I never need to clean my panels?**
No. Steep panels still accumulate dust, pollen, and monsoon grime, just at a slower pace than flatter ones. They still need a regular cleaning schedule, not none at all.
