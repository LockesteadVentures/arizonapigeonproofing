The clearest signs of pigeons under solar panels are cooing sounds at dawn and dusk, droppings collecting on the ground or roofline below the array, feathers and twigs caught at the panel edges, scratching noises from the roof, and a musty or ammonia-like smell near the vents. Reduced panel output can follow, but it shows up weeks after the birds have already moved in, so don't wait for your electric bill to tell you what your ears and nose already know.

Solar arrays create a perfect gap for pigeons: shade, a flat surface underneath, and just enough clearance to walk in and build a nest without a hawk seeing them do it. Here's what to actually look and listen for, room by room and sense by sense.

## The 6-point checklist

### 1. Cooing at dawn and dusk

Pigeons are loudest right after sunrise and again in the last hour before dark, that low, repetitive coo-COO-coo that carries through a bedroom window. If you're hearing it from the direction of your roof and not from a neighbor's yard or a nearby power line, it's worth walking outside and looking up. A single pair will often nest under the same section of panels for months, so the sound becomes a fixture before you notice anything else.

### 2. Droppings on the ground or lower roofline

Look at the ground directly under the array, the gutter line, and any lower roof section the panels overhang. Fresh droppings are a chalky white-and-gray splatter, sometimes still wet-looking; older buildup dries into a crusted, flaking layer that can stain concrete, pavers, and stucco. A few spots from a bird passing through is normal. A concentrated patch that keeps reappearing in the same footprint means something is roosting directly above it, not just perching for a minute.

### 3. Feathers and nesting debris caught at panel edges

Pigeons build loose, messy nests out of twigs, dry palm fronds, and whatever litter is handy, and a lot of that material never makes it fully under the panel. Check the exposed edges and the gap between the panel and the roof for small gray-and-white feathers, straw, or bits of debris snagged on the mounting rails. If you see this at more than one panel, you likely have more than one bird involved.

### 4. Scratching or shuffling sounds from the roof

This one's easy to write off as a tree branch or the roof "settling" in the heat. But a repeated shuffling, scratching sound, especially in the early morning or right after sunset when pigeons are moving around before settling in for the night, is a strong tell. It's most noticeable in a room directly under the array, particularly if your attic space runs close to that section of roof.

### 5. A musty or ammonia smell near roof vents

Accumulated droppings give off a sharp, ammonia-forward smell as they break down, especially after monsoon humidity or an especially hot stretch. If you notice it near an attic vent, a swamp cooler intake, or when you're up on a ladder near the array, that's droppings concentrating somewhere you can't see directly. This is also the sign most tied to actual health risk, since dried droppings can aerosolize dust that carries fungal spores.

### 6. Reduced panel output (a lagging indicator)

Droppings, feathers, and nesting material block sunlight and can trap heat and debris against the panel surface, which drags down output. The catch: this shows up on your utility bill or monitoring app weeks after the birds settled in, and it's easy to blame on dust, monsoon grime, or just a hot month. Treat a real dip in production as confirmation of a problem you should have already caught with sight, sound, or smell, not as your first warning sign.

## What to do if you check these boxes

One or two signs, especially cooing alone, might just mean pigeons are scouting the spot. Three or more, especially droppings plus smell plus sound, means they've likely settled in and started a nest. At that point, don't climb up and start pulling out debris yourself. Removing an active nest without sealing the space behind it just resets the clock. Pigeons are persistent, and if the gap is still open next week, they're often back.

## FAQ

**How fast do pigeons build a nest under solar panels?**
A pair can have a workable nest in a few days once they've chosen a spot, though they'll often keep adding material for weeks. That's why catching the early signs, cooing and scouting behavior, matters more than waiting for droppings to pile up.

**Can I just hose off the droppings and call it fixed?**
Cleaning the mess helps with smell and appearance, but it doesn't address the birds themselves or the gap they're using to get under the array. If the entry point stays open, pigeons typically return to the same spot.

**Is it normal to see just one or two droppings and nothing else?**
Yes. Birds land and pass through roof edges all the time without nesting. A single dropping or two isn't cause for concern on its own; a repeating patch in the same spot, paired with sound or smell, is the real signal.

**Do these signs mean I definitely have an infestation, not just one bird?**
Not necessarily. A lone pigeon can produce a surprising amount of noise and mess. The number of nests matters more for how big a job it is, which is something a crew can assess accurately once they're up on the roof.
