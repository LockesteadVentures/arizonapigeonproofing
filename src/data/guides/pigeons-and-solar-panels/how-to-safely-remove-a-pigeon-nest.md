Remove a pigeon nest only after confirming it's inactive — no eggs, no chicks, no adult sitting tight. Wear a mask, gloves, and eye protection if you do it yourself, because the dried droppings under a solar array can carry histoplasmosis spores and the nesting material is often loaded with mites. And removal without sealing the gap underneath is a wasted afternoon — the same birds, or new ones, will be back within days.

That's the short version. Here's what actually goes into doing it right.

## Why Timing Is the First Decision You Make

Pigeons don't ask permission before nesting under a solar array, and once eggs are laid or chicks have hatched, pulling that nest becomes a different kind of job — ethically and practically. An active nest with eggs or flightless chicks should be left alone until the young have fledged. It's more humane, and it avoids a mess you don't want: displaced chicks dying under a panel, or an agitated adult pigeon repeatedly returning to a half-torn nest.

Here's a fact worth knowing, framed carefully: pigeons (rock doves) are generally not protected under the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act in the United States, unlike most native songbirds. That's general information, not legal advice, and it doesn't mean every state or municipal rule works the same way. If you're not sure what's actually up there — sometimes what looks like a pigeon nest turns out to belong to a dove, sparrow, or something a homeowner shouldn't touch — get a wildlife professional to confirm species and activity status before anyone reaches under a panel. Confirming first costs you a few minutes. Guessing wrong doesn't.

## The Health Risk Nobody Mentions Until It's Too Late

The reason this is worth hiring out isn't the nest itself. It's what's built up underneath it. Pigeon droppings that have dried and accumulated for months, sometimes years, under a solar array can harbor the fungus that causes histoplasmosis — a respiratory infection you get from inhaling disturbed dust, not from touching the droppings. Solar panels create exactly the kind of low-airflow, shaded pocket where that dust sits undisturbed until someone comes along and stirs it up.

On top of that, pigeon nests are practically a mite habitat. Pigeon mites don't stay loyal to their host — once a nest is disturbed, they'll happily crawl onto whoever's doing the disturbing, and they'll bite. Anyone who's pulled a nest by hand and spent the next two days itching around their wrists and forearms can confirm that part.

This combination — respiratory risk from above, mites from direct contact — is exactly why a lot of Tucson homeowners who'd happily clean their own gutters call a crew for this particular job instead. It's not that it's technically hard. It's that the exposure isn't worth it for a task that takes a trained crew twenty minutes.

## If You're Doing It Yourself Anyway

Some homeowners will do it themselves regardless, and if that's the call, do it protected:

- **N95 mask or better** — dust masks alone don't filter fine particulate well enough for dried droppings work.
- **Gloves** — nitrile or heavy work gloves, not the thin disposable kind that tear on debris.
- **Eye protection** — sealed goggles, not open safety glasses, since dust kicks up and drifts under a panel more than you'd expect.
- Bag nesting material directly, don't shake it out over the roof, and wash exposed clothing separately afterward.

Work slowly. Panels get hot enough in a Tucson summer to make careful movement difficult, and rushing under an array with limited clearance is how people scrape hands or crack a panel edge.

## The Part That Actually Matters: Sealing the Gap

Here's the piece that gets skipped constantly. A homeowner pulls the nest, hoses off the mess, and calls it done — then wonders three weeks later why there's a new nest in the exact same spot. Pigeons return to a site because the physical conditions that made it attractive in the first place are still there: the gap between the panel and the roof, the shade, the flat surface underneath. Removing the nest changes nothing about the gap itself.

That gap has to be closed off with something that actually holds up — not loose netting draped over the edge, which pigeons work their way around within a season, but a properly fitted mesh barrier sealing the underside of the array. That's a mechanical fix, not a one-time cleanup, and it's the difference between removing a nest once and removing it every spring.

## FAQ

**Is it legal to remove a pigeon nest from my solar panels?**
Pigeons are generally not protected under the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act in the U.S., unlike most native birds, but this is general information, not legal advice, and local rules can vary. If you're unsure whether the nest is active or which species built it, confirm with a wildlife professional before removing it.

**Can I get sick from removing a pigeon nest myself?**
Yes, that's the main risk. Dried pigeon droppings that get disturbed can release spores that cause histoplasmosis, a respiratory infection, and the nesting material often carries mites that will bite whoever handles it. A mask, gloves, and eye protection reduce but don't eliminate the exposure.

**Why did pigeons come back after I removed the nest?**
Because the nest wasn't the problem — the gap under the panel is. Pigeons return to the same shaded, sheltered spot unless that gap is physically sealed with a proper mesh barrier.

**How do I know if a pigeon nest is active before removing it?**
Watch for eggs, chicks, or an adult consistently sitting in place rather than just perching nearby. If there's any doubt, wait a few days and observe, or ask a wildlife professional to check before disturbing it.
