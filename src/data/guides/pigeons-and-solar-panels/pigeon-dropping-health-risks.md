Pigeon droppings carry a real but often overstated health risk. The main concern is histoplasmosis, a fungal infection that can grow in soil enriched by accumulated droppings and become airborne as dust when disturbed. For most healthy people the risk from casual exposure is low, but dried droppings, active nests, and their mite populations are still worth handling carefully, and older adults or anyone with a weakened immune system should be more cautious. This is general health information, not medical advice.

## Histoplasmosis: the fungal risk in dried droppings

Histoplasmosis comes from a fungus, *Histoplasma capsulatum*, that lives in soil contaminated by bird or bat droppings over time — it isn't something that grows directly in fresh droppings on your roof. The fungus needs droppings to accumulate and dry out, often mixed into dust, debris, or soil, before it becomes a real inhalation hazard. That's why the highest-risk scenario isn't walking past a few splatters on a patio. It's disturbing a large, old, dried-out accumulation — sweeping it, pressure-washing it dry, or scraping it out from under a solar array without wetting it down first — which can kick spores into the air where they get inhaled.

Most healthy adults exposed to a small amount either don't get infected or have mild, flu-like symptoms that resolve on their own. It's a genuine consideration, not a reason to panic every time a pigeon lands on your roof.

## Respiratory and allergy irritation

Separate from histoplasmosis, dried dropping dust is simply an irritant. Proteins in the dust can trigger sneezing, coughing, watery eyes, or worse symptoms in people with asthma or existing respiratory conditions. This is the more common complaint homeowners actually report — it shows up as irritation near an attic vent, a swamp cooler intake, or wherever droppings have built up close to airflow into the house, not usually as a diagnosed illness.

## Mites and parasites from an active nest

An active pigeon nest is its own problem. Pigeons host several types of mites and occasionally fleas or ticks, and when a nest is abandoned or the birds move on, those parasites can migrate looking for a new host — sometimes into nearby living space through a vent gap or roofline. This is one of the more unpleasant, under-discussed parts of a pigeon problem: it's not just the mess on the panels, it's what was living in the nest underneath them.

## Who should be more cautious

Arizona has a large retiree population, and that's directly relevant here. Older adults, young children, and anyone who is immunocompromised — whether from a chronic condition, cancer treatment, or an organ transplant — face a higher risk of complications if histoplasmosis exposure does occur. If you fall into one of those groups, or care for someone who does, it's reasonable to be more careful about disturbing dried droppings yourself and to lean toward having someone else handle it.

## FAQ

**Can you get histoplasmosis just from being near pigeon droppings?**
Casual proximity — walking by, having a few droppings on a windowsill — is low risk for most healthy people. The real exposure risk comes from disturbing dried, accumulated droppings so the dust becomes airborne and gets inhaled.

**Are pigeon droppings dangerous to touch?**
Touching dried droppings isn't the main pathway for histoplasmosis; inhaling disturbed dust is. Still, wash your hands after any contact and avoid touching your face, and don't dry-sweep or brush off large accumulations.

**Do all pigeon nests have mites?**
Not every nest, but active or recently abandoned pigeon nests commonly host mites and other parasites. It's common enough that professional removal treats it as a standard precaution.

**Is this something to worry about for a small amount of droppings on my patio?**
Generally no — small, everyday amounts on hard surfaces are a cleaning issue, not a health emergency. The concern scales up with the size and age of the accumulation, particularly a built-up nest.
