Pigeons nest under solar panels because the raised array creates a shaded, wind-blocked gap that stays cooler than the surrounding roof in summer and warmer at night — with a solid "ceiling" overhead that keeps hawks and owls out. In Arizona, that gap also doubles as shelter from monsoon downpours and triple-digit sun, which is exactly what a nesting pigeon is looking for. Once a pair finds it, they'll return to the same spot to raise multiple broods a year unless the space is sealed off.

## The gap under your panels is basically a pigeon condo

Solar installers mount panels a few inches above the roof deck so air can circulate and cool the cells. That gap is an accident of engineering, not a design flaw, but pigeons read it as prime real estate. Three things make it work so well for them:

**Shade.** A Tucson roof in July can hit temperatures that would cook an egg. The underside of a panel blocks direct sun almost entirely, keeping that gap noticeably cooler than open shingle or tile.

**Insulation.** At night, the panel holds onto residual heat and acts like a lid, keeping the space warmer than the open roof. For a bird trying to keep eggs at a stable temperature, that's a free incubator.

**Predator safety.** Pigeons are prey animals first. A flat surface a few inches overhead means nothing can swoop down from above, and the tight side clearance keeps out anything bigger than a bird. From a hawk's perspective, or a roof rat's, the nest simply isn't reachable.

Put those three together and you've got a space that's cooler in the heat, warmer at night, dry, and defensible. Most attics and eaves offer maybe one of those. A solar array offers all three at once, which is why roofs with panels attract pigeons that would otherwise nest somewhere else entirely.

## Why this is worse in Arizona specifically

Every one of those advantages gets amplified by the desert climate.

Arizona pigeons are heat-seeking by necessity. Feral and rock pigeons here spend the hottest parts of the day hunting for any surface that isn't radiating heat back at them, and a shaded panel gap is one of the few spots on a residential roof that qualifies. Birds that might tolerate an exposed ledge in a milder climate won't tolerate one in a Tucson summer, so the panel gap becomes disproportionately attractive here compared to a house somewhere cooler.

Monsoon season adds a second reason. From roughly June through September, Southern Arizona gets sudden, heavy downpours with wind gusts strong enough to drive rain sideways. A solid panel overhead is one of the only spots on an open roof that stays fully dry and out of the wind during a storm. Pigeons that have already scoped out the gap as a heat shelter treat it as storm shelter too, and a bird that's used a spot successfully once tends to keep coming back.

That combination — daytime heat relief plus monsoon cover — means Arizona roofs see pigeons investigate the space under panels earlier in the season and more persistently than roofs in cooler climates. Arizona pigeons don't behave differently as a species. The local climate just makes this particular gap more valuable to them than almost anywhere else they could nest.

## Why prevention beats removal every time

Nesting material, droppings, and feather debris under a panel don't just create a mess. Droppings are acidic and corrosive over time, debris can block airflow the panel needs to run efficiently, and once eggs or chicks are present, removal gets legally and ethically complicated — you generally have to wait out the nesting cycle before you can clear it. That can mean months of reduced panel output and roof damage while you wait.

The mechanics above explain why the gap is attractive in the first place, which is also exactly why sealing it off before pigeons move in works so much better than dealing with them afterward. Mesh installed around the perimeter of the array removes the shade-and-shelter advantage entirely without touching the panels themselves or voiding the installer's warranty when done correctly.

## FAQ

**Do pigeons damage solar panels by nesting under them?**
Indirectly, yes. The panels themselves usually survive, but droppings are acidic and can degrade roofing material and wiring nearby, nesting debris can restrict the airflow that keeps panels running efficiently, and the added weight and moisture from nests can accelerate wear on roof surfaces underneath.

**How do I know if pigeons are already living under my panels?**
Look for cooing at dawn, feathers or twigs caught at the panel edges, and droppings streaking down from the gap. Our [signs of pigeons under solar panels guide](/guides/pigeons-and-solar-panels/signs-of-pigeons-under-solar-panels/) covers this in more detail.

**Can I just block the gap myself with a tarp or netting?**
You can try, but draped netting and DIY barriers tend to sag, gap, or blow loose in monsoon wind, which gives pigeons an opening right back in. A custom-fit mesh install seals the actual perimeter of the array instead of just covering it.

**Is it legal to remove a pigeon nest from under my panels?**
Once eggs or chicks are present, removal is generally restricted until the nesting cycle finishes, which is a strong reason to seal the gap before birds move in rather than after.
