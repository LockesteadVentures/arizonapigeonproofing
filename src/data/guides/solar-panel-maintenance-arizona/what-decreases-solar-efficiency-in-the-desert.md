Solar production drops in Arizona for one of six reasons: the heat itself (panels lose efficiency as they get hotter, and Tucson roofs run hot), a film of dust and pollen sitting on the glass, pigeons roosting and leaving droppings and nesting debris, monsoon storms depositing grit instead of washing it away, ordinary panel aging over the years, or an inverter that's underperforming or has failed outright. Most homeowners assume it's one obvious thing. Usually it's two of these stacked on top of each other, and figuring out which two is what actually gets your output back.

This piece walks through all six so you can figure out which apply to your roof, then points you to the deeper guide for whichever one fits.

## Heat: the one cause that isn't dirt or wildlife

Here's the part that surprises people: solar panels get less efficient as they get hotter, and that's basic photovoltaic physics, not a defect. Panels are rated at a standard test temperature of 77°F. Every degree above that, output drops a bit, and a panel sitting on a Tucson roof in July can run 40 or 50 degrees hotter than that rating. That's a real, unavoidable efficiency hit that has nothing to do with dust, birds, or maintenance, and no amount of cleaning fixes it. Good installers account for some of this in system sizing, but it's worth knowing it exists so you don't mistake a hot-August dip in your production app for a problem you can service away.

## Dust and pollen: the slow, even fade

Arizona is dry almost all year, with dust storms rolling through and heavy mesquite, palo verde, and olive pollen blanketing everything each spring. A widely cited NREL finding is that rain alone doesn't reliably clear this kind of buildup off solar panels — a storm often just wets the film and redistributes it rather than washing it away. In wetter climates that matters less because it rains often enough to get a partial reset regularly. In Tucson, weeks or months can pass with no rain at all, so whatever settles keeps settling.

The signature of this cause is a gradual, even decline in production with no single dramatic cause. ## Pigeons: droppings and nesting debris

Pigeons like the gap under a tilted solar panel for the same reason you'd like shade on a 105-degree day — it's cooler, dry, and hidden from predators. Once a pair nests there, droppings accumulate on and around the array in thick, uneven clumps rather than a uniform haze. That unevenness matters: a heavily shaded cell in a string can run hotter than its neighbors, a different and often worse problem than an even layer of grime. Nesting material can also clog drainage paths and, in some cases, damage wiring insulation, which isn't covered under a standard solar warranty because it's classified as pest damage.

The tell here isn't just a production dip — it's cooing or scratching at dawn, feathers in the gutters, or visible streaking down the roofline below the array. ## Monsoon season: storms that add grime instead of removing it

Counterintuitively, monsoon season often makes soiling worse, not better. Storm runoff carries dust and grit up onto panel glass and leaves it there once the water evaporates, frequently as a streaked, uneven film that looks worse than the dry dust that was there before the rain. Homeowners who assume "at least the summer storms are rinsing things off" are usually wrong, which is exactly why we schedule cleanings around monsoon season rather than a generic calendar.

## Panel aging: the decline you can't clean away

Even a spotless, bird-free array loses a small amount of output every year simply from the panels aging — cell materials degrade gradually over decades of sun exposure, which is why manufacturers publish degradation curves in their warranties. This is normal, not a sign anything is wrong. It shows up as a slow year-over-year trend across your system's whole history, not a sudden or seasonal dip tied to a specific event.

## Inverter issues: when the problem isn't the panels at all

Sometimes the panels are fine and the inverter is the bottleneck — converting DC to AC less efficiently than it should, throwing intermittent faults, or failing outright on one string while the rest of the array keeps reporting normally. A sudden drop, or an error code in your monitoring app, points toward the inverter or wiring rather than anything sitting on the glass. That's worth a call to your installer or the inverter manufacturer directly, since it's outside what a cleaning or pigeon-proofing visit addresses.

## So which one is yours?

A quick gut check: sudden and tied to an error code points to the inverter. Gradual and even across the whole array points to dust, pollen, or plain aging. Uneven, concentrated, or paired with noise and streaking points to pigeons. Worse right after a storm points to monsoon soiling. And a dip that tracks the outdoor thermometer more than the calendar is probably just heat.

## FAQ

**Why is my solar production dropping in Arizona if I haven't changed anything?**
The most common causes are dust and pollen film, pigeon droppings and nesting debris, monsoon storms redepositing grit, ordinary panel aging, heat itself reducing cell efficiency, or an inverter issue. Most of these build up gradually, so a drop can show up even though nothing about your system changed on your end.

**Does heat alone reduce solar panel output, even on clean panels?**
Yes. Panels are rated at 77°F and lose efficiency as they run hotter, which is unavoidable physics rather than a maintenance issue. A Tucson roof in summer routinely pushes panels well above that rating, so some seasonal dip in the hottest months is normal even on a spotless, bird-free array.

**How do I tell dust buildup apart from a pigeon problem?**
Dust settles as an even haze across the whole panel and causes a gradual, uniform decline. Pigeon droppings land in concentrated clumps, often paired with cooing, scratching, feathers in the gutters, or streaking down the roofline. If you're seeing both signs, you likely have both problems.

**Could the issue be my inverter instead of the panels?**
It's possible, especially if the drop was sudden rather than gradual, or your monitoring app shows a fault code. Inverter issues aren't something cleaning or pigeon-proofing will fix, so that's worth a direct call to your installer or the inverter manufacturer.
