A complete Arizona solar panel maintenance checklist covers six things: seasonal cleaning timed around monsoon season, monthly checks of your monitoring app's production numbers, a visual inspection for pigeons and nesting debris, a post-storm damage check, awareness of what's electrical versus what's not, and an annual professional inspection. Run through it twice a year at minimum — spring and fall — and after every major monsoon storm. Below is the full checklist, section by section, with what to look for and why it matters in the Tucson desert specifically.

## Seasonal cleaning: before and after monsoon

Dust and pollen film build on panel glass year-round in Tucson, but monsoon (roughly June through September) is when timing matters most.

- [ ] **Before monsoon (May–early June):** Clean panels so the array starts the season at full output, not already carrying a spring pollen film into the wet months.
- [ ] **After monsoon (September–October):** Schedule a second cleaning. This is the one homeowners skip, and the one that matters more. NREL has found that rain alone doesn't reliably clear dust and pollen off solar glass — a monsoon downpour can actually streak buildup into a film that clings harder than the dry dust did.
- [ ] **Spot-check after big dust events:** A dust storm or "haboob" rolling through is worth a quick look to decide if an off-cycle cleaning is warranted.

For the deeper mechanism on why heat, dust, and pollen cut into output here more than in most climates, see [what decreases solar efficiency in the desert](/guides/solar-panel-maintenance-arizona/what-decreases-solar-efficiency-in-the-desert/).

## Monitoring app production checks

Most residential solar systems ship with a monitoring app. Use it — it's the fastest, cheapest maintenance tool a homeowner has, and it requires no ladder.

- [ ] **Check monthly**, not just when the bill looks off. A slow decline is easy to miss otherwise.
- [ ] **Compare against the same month last year**, not last month. Output swings seasonally with day length and sun angle, so year-over-year, same month, is the real signal.
- [ ] **Watch for a drop isolated to one panel or string** rather than the whole array. That points to something localized — shade, debris, or nesting — rather than a system-wide issue like dirty glass.
- [ ] **Flag anything you can't explain.** No dust storm, no new shade, no bird activity, but output still dropped — that's worth a professional look rather than a guess.

## Visual inspection: pigeons, nesting, and droppings

This is the check most homeowners never think to do until there's already a problem, and it's the one most specific to Arizona roofs. Pigeons treat the gap under a panel array as a shaded, wind-protected nesting spot, and Tucson rooftops give them plenty of options.

- [ ] **Look for droppings** on the roof, panel edges, or ground below the array — usually the first visible sign, often before you hear or see the birds.
- [ ] **Listen at dawn.** Pigeons are most active early morning; cooing and movement under the panels is easier to catch before the day heats up.
- [ ] **Check for nesting material** (twigs, feathers, debris) at panel edges or gaps between panel and roof.
- [ ] **Note any panel running hotter or dirtier than its neighbors.** Droppings and nesting debris trap heat and block sunlight unevenly, which shows up as a localized dip in your monitoring app.

Don't treat this as a cleaning-only problem. Droppings come back as long as birds have access underneath the array — the fix is sealing the gap, not just washing what's there. ## Physical damage check after storms

Arizona monsoon storms bring wind gusts, occasional hail, and debris — all worth a look after any storm that made you nervous watching from the window.

- [ ] **Scan for cracked or chipped glass**, especially after hail or wind-driven debris.
- [ ] **Check mounting and racking for looseness** — a panel shifted slightly, or edges less flush than before.
- [ ] **Look for bent or lifted flashing** where the array meets the roofline — a common entry point for water and for pigeons if it opens a gap.
- [ ] **Check for debris caught under or between panels** — palm fronds, mesquite branches, and roofing grit are common after a wind event.
- [ ] **Note anything visibly different from your last inspection**, even if you can't tell what caused it. That's the cue to call a professional rather than investigate further yourself.

## Inverter and wiring: what to watch, and what to leave alone

This is the one part of the checklist where the right move is to look, not touch.

- [ ] **Check the inverter display or app for error codes or an "offline" status.** This is the extent of a homeowner's electrical check — a glance at the readout, not opening a panel or touching wiring.
- [ ] **Note any burning smell, unusual buzzing, or scorch marks** near the inverter or conduit, and treat these as an immediate call to a licensed electrician or your solar installer, not a wait-and-see item.
- [ ] **Do not attempt wiring, connector, or inverter repairs yourself.** Rooftop solar wiring carries real voltage, and DC connections can stay energized even when a breaker is off.

A clear line matters here: Arizona Pigeon Proofing handles cleaning and bird-proofing, not electrical work. ## Vegetation and shade management

Shade is one of the few items on this checklist a homeowner can usually manage without a ladder or a technician.

- [ ] **Track tree growth near the array**, especially fast growers like palo verde or mesquite planted after installation. A tree with no shade impact at install can shade a corner of the array within a few years.
- [ ] **Trim branches casting even partial shade on part of the array.** Depending on your system's wiring, partial shade on one panel can drag down output for an entire string.
- [ ] **Watch for new structures** — a patio cover, a neighbor's second story, anything that changes the shade pattern the array was designed around.

## Annual professional inspection

Everything above is homeowner-level: a look, a listen, a check of an app. Once a year, it's worth having someone who does this for a living take a closer, hands-on look.

- [ ] **Schedule one professional visit per year** that combines a cleaning with a full visual inspection of mounting, mesh (if installed), and panel condition.
- [ ] **Ask for before-and-after photos** of the inspection, not just a verbal "looks fine."
- [ ] **Use this visit to catch small issues early** — a loosening mesh seam, early droppings, a hairline crack — before they turn into a bigger repair or a bigger pigeon problem.

## FAQ

**How often should I run through a solar panel maintenance checklist in Arizona?**
Twice a year at minimum — once before monsoon season in late spring, once after it in early fall — plus a quick check after any major storm. Monitoring app checks should happen monthly.

**What maintenance can I do myself versus what needs a professional?**
Visual checks are homeowner-level: monitoring app review, looking for droppings or nesting, scanning for storm damage, and watching for shade from growing trees. Cleaning, bird-proofing, and anything electrical (inverter faults, wiring, connectors) should go to a professional — cleaning and bird-proofing to a company like ours, electrical issues to a licensed electrician or your solar installer.

**What's the single most commonly skipped item on this checklist?**
The post-monsoon cleaning. Homeowners often clean before monsoon season and assume the rain handles the rest. It doesn't — NREL's research shows rain alone doesn't reliably clear dust and pollen, and it can even streak buildup into a harder film.

**Do I need a professional inspection every year even if nothing seems wrong?**
Yes, if you want to catch small issues before they become expensive ones. A loosening mesh seam or a hairline crack rarely shows up in your monitoring app until it's already cost you output for weeks.
