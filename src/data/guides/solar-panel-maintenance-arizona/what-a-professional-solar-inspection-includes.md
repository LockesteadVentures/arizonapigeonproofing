A professional solar panel inspection from a cleaning-and-bird-proofing company covers what's visible and reachable from the roof: soiling and staining on the glass, signs of pigeon or bird activity, the condition of any mesh or deterrent already installed, storm or debris damage, and a look at your recent production trends if you have the data handy. It does not include inverter diagnostics, wiring checks, or anything behind an electrical panel — that's a licensed electrician's or your original installer's job, not this one. Knowing the difference up front means you'll get a useful visit instead of a disappointing one.

That distinction matters enough to say plainly before anything else: this is not an electrical safety inspection. It's a physical, visual inspection focused on the two things pigeons and dust actually break — output and hardware.

## The visual soiling check

The technician starts by looking at the glass itself. Tucson dust settles unevenly, and a panel that looks "a little dusty" from the ground can be carrying enough film to measurably cut output, especially after a dry stretch with no rain to even partially rinse it. NREL research has found that rainfall alone isn't reliable for clearing dust and pollen off solar panels, so a visual check often catches buildup a homeowner assumed the last monsoon storm handled.

Staining gets separate attention from plain dust. Mineral staining, bird droppings, and sap or leaf residue all behave differently and call for different cleaning approaches, so this part of the inspection is really diagnosing what's on the glass, not just confirming that something is.

## Checking for pigeon and bird activity

This is the part most other cleaning companies skip, and it's usually the more important half of the visit. The technician looks for droppings on the roof or ground beneath the array, nesting material wedged at panel edges, feathers, and the telltale staining pattern that shows up under a panel that's been used as a roost for a while.

It's not just about what's there now. A panel with a clean surface today can still have an active nest tucked underneath it, invisible from a quick glance but obvious once someone gets a flashlight under the edge. Catching that early is the difference between a bird-proofing job and a bird-proofing-plus-wiring-damage job.

## Mounting hardware and mesh condition

If your array already has mesh or another deterrent installed — whether from Arizona Pigeon Proofing or another company — the inspection checks whether it's still doing its job. Mesh can work loose at the edges over time, especially after a windy monsoon season, and a gap that's opened up an inch is all a pigeon needs to get back in. The technician also checks visible mounting hardware and racking for looseness or shifting, since that's often the first sign something moved during a storm.

## Storm and debris damage

Wind, hail, and blowing debris are routine in a Tucson summer, and this part of the inspection is a straightforward look for what changed. That includes cracked or chipped glass, bent or lifted flashing where the array meets the roofline, and palm fronds, mesquite branches, or roofing grit caught under or between panels. None of this requires special equipment — just someone who knows what a normal panel looks like and what doesn't belong.

## Reviewing your production trends, if you have them

Most residential systems came with a monitoring app, and if you can pull up recent numbers, the technician will look at them alongside what they see on the roof. A slow decline that lines up with visible soiling points to a cleaning fix. A drop isolated to one panel or string, with nothing obviously wrong on the surface, is a different conversation — sometimes it's shade or debris, sometimes it's a sign to loop in the installer.

## What's explicitly out of scope, and why

Here's the line, stated plainly: this inspection does not cover the inverter, wiring, breakers, or anything behind an access panel. That's not a corner being cut — it's a scope boundary, and a company that quietly steps past it into electrical work without a license is doing you a disservice, not a favor. Diagnosing inverter error codes, tracing a wiring fault, or evaluating whether a system is electrically safe belongs to a licensed electrician or the company that originally installed your system. If anything during the visual check points toward an electrical issue — a burning smell, scorch marks, unusual buzzing — the right move is a direct referral, not a guess.

## What to do with what you learn

An inspection is only useful if it ends with a clear next step. If the visit turns up dust and staining, that's a case for scheduling a cleaning. If it turns up bird activity or gaps in existing mesh, that's a bird-proofing conversation. Either way, you'll know which problem you actually have instead of assuming. ## FAQ

**Does a solar panel inspection include checking the inverter?**
No. A cleaning-and-bird-proofing inspection is visual and physical — soiling, bird activity, mesh condition, and storm damage. Inverter diagnostics and wiring checks require a licensed electrician or your original installer.

**How long does a professional solar inspection take?**
It varies with array size and roof access, but most residential inspections are quick, often folded into the same visit as a cleaning or bird-proofing quote rather than scheduled as a separate appointment.

**What if the inspection finds something electrical?**
You'll get a direct referral to a licensed electrician or your installer. This company won't attempt electrical diagnostics or repairs, and you shouldn't want a bird-proofing crew touching your wiring anyway.

**Do I need to have monitoring app data ready for the inspection?**
It helps but isn't required. If you have recent production numbers, the technician can compare them against what they find on the roof. If not, the visual inspection stands on its own.
